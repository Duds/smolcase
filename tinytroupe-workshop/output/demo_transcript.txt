[Jordan Okafor]
I'll give you the concrete scenario, because I've lived the human version of it. Picture a payments platform, 2 a.m., a queue backs up. There's a junior engineer on the bridge with me — smart, six months in. Messages need replaying. The load-bearing question is: 'is it safe to replay these?' And the honest answer is 'it depends on whether every consumer downstream is idempotent,' which is the kind of thing that lives in tribal knowledge and one undocumented assumption buried in a service nobody's touched in two years.

Now give that engineer this tool. They ask, 'is it safe to replay these messages?' The tool scans the code, sees retries and a dedupe check in the main consumer, and answers — fluently, in well-structured English — 'yes, the pipeline is designed to be idempotent.' And it'll be *almost* right. Right about four out of five consumers. The fifth one isn't, and now we've double-charged customers, and the post-mortem starts with an engineer saying 'but the tool said it was safe,' and the tool said it with perfect grammar.

That's the failure mode exactly: the tool fails precisely where human experts fail — on the undocumented edge case — but a human expert hedges. A senior engineer says 'I *think* it's safe, but check the billing consumer.' The tool has no hedge instinct unless someone engineered it in. Fluent prose reads as calibrated confidence, and juniors don't yet have the scar tissue to know which questions are load-bearing. That's the part that worries me — not that it errs, but that it errs in a register that signals authority.

On the flip side — and I'll genuinely concede this — is plausible-but-imperfect better than nothing? Sometimes, yes. If I'm dropped into a codebase I've never seen and I just need a rough map — 'where does the retry logic live, roughly?' — an imperfect answer that gets me to the right directory beats forty minutes of grep. The difference is whether the output is a *starting point for verification* or a *substitute for it*. Map, fine. Verdict, dangerous. So my line isn't 'don't build it' — it's that the tool needs to make the distinction structural: citations to file and line, visible uncertainty, 'I found a dedupe check in these four consumers, I can't verify the fifth.' If it says *that*, I'm a buyer. If it just says 'yes, it's safe,' I'd rather it stayed silent.
